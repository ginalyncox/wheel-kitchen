05 — Safety

Fires happen fast. Response has to be planned before you need it.

The most authoritative resource here is the FEW Program — Fire Readiness for Wheelchair Users — developed at the University of Illinois with the Craig H. Neilsen Foundation. Most home fire-safety curricula assume the resident can walk quickly to an exit. FEW is one of the few that doesn't.

This page describes general safety principles drawn from published fire-safety curricula. It is not a substitute for a personalized emergency plan created with your local fire department or occupational therapist. Many U.S. fire departments will do a free home safety visit — call the non-emergency line.

Preventing a kitchen fire — the wheelchair-specific version

National kitchen-fire statistics point to unattended cooking as the single largest cause. For wheelchair users the risk profile is slightly different: you may be more likely to leave a stove briefly to retrieve something you couldn't reach at first, and the trip back takes longer than it would for a standing cook. The prevention rules that matter:

  • Never leave the kitchen while a burner is on. Not for the phone. Not for the door. If you have to leave, turn the burner off first. This is the single most important habit.
  • Set a timer for anything in the oven. Loud phone timer, kitchen timer clipped to your chair, or smart-speaker timer. "I'll remember" is how things burn.
  • Wear tight sleeves or roll them up. Loose cotton catches easily, and from a seated position your sleeves pass directly over the burners.
  • Keep everything flammable off the stove surface. Wooden utensils, paper towels, oven mitts, spice jars — all off the range when it's on. The stove is not a shelf.
  • Front burner rule. As covered in Stovetop & oven: use the front burners whenever possible so you're not reaching over hot pans.
  • Keep a working smoke alarm within earshot of the kitchen — not in the kitchen, where it will nuisance-alarm and you'll disable it. Test it monthly.

If a small fire starts

The response depends on what is burning. Order the tools by the front of the stove before you cook, not after a fire has already started.

Pan fire (grease, oil, food in a pan)

  1. Turn the burner off.
  2. Cover the pan with a lid. Slide a metal lid (not glass) from the side over the top. This suffocates the fire.
  3. Do not move the pan. Do not carry a burning pan to the sink. Leave it on the burner with the lid on until it's cold.
  4. Never pour water on a grease fire. Water on burning oil creates a fireball.
  5. Baking soda works on a small grease fire if a lid isn't handy — keep a large open box next to the stove.

Oven fire

  1. Keep the oven door closed. Turn the oven off. Starving the fire of oxygen usually smothers it.
  2. Do not open the door to look. Opening feeds it air.
  3. If it doesn't self-extinguish in a minute, evacuate and call 911.

Anything else

Paper towel, dish towel, small package — pull it off the heat source (with tongs, not your hands), drop it in the sink, run water on it.

If it grows past small — a wheelchair-specific rule

Standard fire-safety guidance is "if the fire doesn't go out in 30 seconds, get out and call 911." For a wheelchair user, that timeline should be shorter, because your exit takes longer:

  • If you're not confident you can extinguish it in 15 seconds, leave.
  • Close doors behind you as you exit — this slows the fire's spread and buys the fire department time.
  • Call 911 from outside, not from the kitchen.
  • Tell dispatch that you use a wheelchair. Fire departments dispatch differently when a resident may need assistance evacuating.
Fire extinguishers — the honest version

A kitchen-rated ABC or K-class extinguisher is worth having, mounted at reachable height near the exit — not near the stove, where you can't get to it once the fire is between you and it. But operating one one-handed while managing a chair is hard, and the extinguisher weight (5–10 lb) makes it awkward. The lid-on-pan technique is more reliable for grease fires than an extinguisher for most wheelchair users. Keep both; reach for the lid first.

Burns — prevention and first aid

Wheelchair users, especially those with sensation loss from SCI, MS, or diabetic neuropathy, are at elevated burn risk. From the Urban Cripple stovetop guide: "This is especially important for people with paralysis or nerve damage that makes it harder to sense extreme temperatures."

Prevention

  • Long silicone forearm mitts — protect the entire arm up to the elbow.
  • Never put hot pans on your lap. Use the rolling cart or a bean-bag lap tray as a barrier.
  • Check the temperature of hot dishwater with your elbow, not your hand — same principle as testing a baby's bath water.
  • Set your water heater to 120°F maximum. Above that, scalding happens in seconds.
  • Do routine skin checks on your legs and lap if you have reduced sensation. A burn you can't feel needs to be found visually.

First aid for a burn

  1. Cool with running water — cool, not cold — for 10 to 20 minutes. This is the single most effective treatment and dramatically reduces long-term damage.
  2. Remove jewelry and tight clothing from around the burn area before it swells.
  3. Do not apply butter, ice, or ointment immediately. Cool water only.
  4. Cover loosely with a clean, non-stick dressing. Plastic wrap works well as a temporary covering.
  5. Seek medical attention if the burn is: larger than the palm of your hand; on the face, hand, foot, or genitals; blistered; deep (looks white or charred); or if you cannot feel it because of sensation loss (means it may be deeper than it appears).

A wheelchair-specific escape plan

The FEW curriculum's core insight is that emergency escape has to be planned before the emergency. Standard fire drills for a wheelchair user look like:

  • Know your two exits from the kitchen. Not "know your two exits from the house." Specifically from the room where cooking happens.
  • Keep both paths clear. A trash can in a doorway is a barrier when you can't step over it.
  • Practice a real evacuation once a year. Time it. If your usual exit route takes more than 30 seconds, that's information.
  • Register with your local fire department. Many jurisdictions maintain a voluntary registry of residents who may need evacuation assistance. Ask your non-emergency line about "special needs" or "functional needs" registries.
  • Sleep on the ground floor if your home has one. Stairwell evacuations are complicated even for able-bodied residents; for a wheelchair user they require rescue.

The FEW Program Manual is a free 60-page resource — the most detailed wheelchair-user fire-safety document I've found. Available at Publish.Illinois.edu (FEW Program).
