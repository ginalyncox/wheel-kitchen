03 — Technique

The technique changes when the height, the leverage, and the reach change.

A seated cook doesn't push down on a knife the same way a standing one does. Doesn't whisk with the same shoulder. Doesn't stir with the same wrist. Different mechanics, different techniques — often better ones.

Non-slip everything

Standing cooks brace with their body against the counter. Seated cooks brace with their hand — which means the other hand is doing all the work while the non-dominant hand is essentially holding the workpiece still. If the workpiece slides, you compensate with force, and that's where you cut yourself.

The base layer of every prep session:

  • Silicone non-slip mat under the cutting board. A basic $8 rectangular silicone baking mat works — grippy on both sides, easy to wash.
  • Non-slip mixing bowl. Either buy a bowl with a silicone base or set any bowl on a wet dish towel. Bowls that dance across the counter are what force you to lean and grip harder.
  • Damp paper towel under measuring cups. Same principle, tiny effort.

Cutting without leverage — sharp knives, small board, choose your grip

A dull knife is worse than a sharp one. It skates on the food, requires more force, and slips. From New Mobility: "A sharp knife is a must, but not so sharp that you wind up with cuts and lots of Band-Aids." Keep one chef's knife sharpened — a cheap pull-through sharpener is fine for home cooking.

  • Cutting board with corner spikes / one-handed cutting board. A "Cook-Helper" style board has stainless-steel spikes to secure a potato or onion while you cut — invaluable if you have limited grip or use one hand. Available from multiple adaptive-kitchen suppliers.
  • Rocking mezzaluna over chopping. A curved mezzaluna blade rocks back and forth on a curved board — it uses gravity, not downward force. Ideal for herbs, garlic, small vegetables.
  • Pizza wheel is a knife. MIND (Malaysia Mental Illness Network) makes this point: a pizza cutter with a large handle slices herbs, soft vegetables, dough, and pancakes with a rolling motion instead of a chopping one. Much less wrist strain.
  • Food processor for real volume. If you're chopping an onion, do it by hand. If you're chopping five onions, use the processor. The trade — a bit of cleanup for a lot of shoulder — is almost always worth it.
The cutting board should be thick enough that a hot frying pan won't burn your lap — but thin enough that it's not so heavy it's a pain to pick up. — accessible-cooking guidance summarized from Living Spinal

One-handed and low-grip prep

For cooks with hemiplegia, limb difference, tremor, or fatigue that varies during a cook, one-handed workflows aren't a workaround — they're the primary technique. The most-cited resource here is United Spinal's Adaptive Cooking Techniques, which recommends:

  • Rocker knives — a curved blade with a vertical handle. You rock through the food with one hand and downward pressure. No sawing motion required.
  • Weighted-handle utensils — heavier handles counteract tremor and require less grip strength. Good Grips (OXO) and Kuhn Rikon make widely-available weighted lines.
  • Utensil handle build-ups — thick foam sleeves (or wrapping rubberized tape) on existing utensils so you don't need a full grip to hold them.
  • Universal cuffs — a strap around the palm with a pocket that holds a utensil. Lets you use a spatula, spoon, or fork with reduced hand function. Available from Flint Rehab and other adaptive-kitchen suppliers.

Mixing without shoulder pain

Whisking a batter or beating eggs by hand at a standing counter is a shoulder-and-elbow workout. Seated, working up-and-in against gravity, it's worse. The three moves:

  • Bring the bowl down. Onto the drawer-and-board prep surface, or onto your lap tray, so your working elbow drops below shoulder height. This single change eliminates most of the strain.
  • Electric hand mixer > whisk for anything beyond a quick emulsion. Cordless models are now cheap; they replace a task that takes minutes of shoulder work with one that takes twenty seconds.
  • Stand mixer parked at seated height. If you bake regularly, this is the tool. Attach a splash guard so you can walk away.

Reaching high and low

A good reacher is not an optional purchase. New Mobility's Katie Rodriguez Banister calls hers "indispensable." Two features matter:

  • Length — 30 to 32 inches. Anything shorter and you're still stretching; anything longer gets unwieldy. Living Unlimited specifically recommends the 32-inch Carex Ultra Grabber.
  • Grip strength that holds a full bottle without slipping. Cheap reachers drop things. Pay for a heavy-duty one with rubberized jaws.

For low reaches (base cabinets, under-sink storage), zip-tie loops on cabinet handles — a tip from Enabling Devices — extend the handle out into a loop you can hook a finger through. Same trick works on the refrigerator door.

Opening jars, cans, and stubborn packages

  • Under-cabinet jar opener. A V-shaped metal grip mounted under a cabinet edge; you slide a jar lid in, twist the jar, the grip holds the lid. Works one-handed.
  • Electric can opener that attaches to and removes the lid without you holding the can. The One-Touch style opens cans hands-free.
  • Silicone jar-opening pad — a $3 grippy disc that gives you enough purchase to open a stiff lid one-handed.
  • Kitchen shears > scissors > teeth for opening packaging. Keep a pair magnetically stuck to the fridge.
