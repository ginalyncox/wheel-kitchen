07 — After

The dishes are half of cooking. Set them up to not defeat you.

A standard sink is 8–10 inches deep with the drain 30+ inches back from the front edge. From a seated position, that's a strain radius that ends most cooks. Some of this you can fix with equipment; some you fix with sequencing.

The sink is the problem — several ways to fix it

The specific issue with standard sinks: the front edge of the counter is at your collarbone height, the sink floor is another 8+ inches below the counter, and reaching in means bending forward, over, and down all at once. Options:

  • Pull-out sprayer / long spray hose. The single most useful sink upgrade — bring the water to the pot on the cart instead of lifting the pot into the sink.
  • Sink caddy or basin insert. A plastic basin that sits inside the sink at a higher level, so you're working 3–4 inches shallower.
  • Wash on the cart, rinse in the sink. Soap and scrub at the rolling cart with a bowl of soapy water; roll to the sink and use the sprayer to rinse. This is the single biggest cleanup workflow change.
  • Dishwasher. If you have one, use it for almost everything. A $30 silicone dishwasher rack accessory lets you wash small items, jar lids, and baby-bottle parts. Even lightweight portable countertop dishwashers exist ($250–$400) if the built-in isn't reachable.
Shallow sinks are worth it

If you ever replace a sink, a shallow (5–6 inch) single-basin sink combined with a long spray hose transforms cleanup. Most bathroom-remodel budgets can absorb a kitchen sink swap; both jobs use the same plumber.

A dishwashing sequence that doesn't beat you up

Order matters. Do them in this order and each step gets easier:

  1. Scrape into the trash at the cart, not at the sink. A small trash can on your lap or on the cart shelf saves the trip.
  2. Rinse everything with the sprayer before scrubbing. Removes the food residue while the plates are still warm.
  3. Fill a plastic tub or the sink with hot soapy water, and let everything soak while you wipe counters. Ten minutes of soak = half the scrubbing.
  4. Wash from cleanest to dirtiest. Glasses, silverware, plates, bowls, pans. The pan is last, when your energy might be lowest — but it's also the item you can honestly leave for later.
  5. Rinse into the drying rack. No towel-drying pass required.

Drying without stacking

A big wet stack of dishes is an ergonomic disaster to put away. Two changes:

  • Wide, shallow drying rack over a drain mat (not a stacking two-tier). Items dry in a single layer and put away in a single motion.
  • Put dishes away directly from the rack, not via a "dry stack" pile. One handling, not two. The extra transfer is where drops happen.

The big pot

The one thing that consistently defeats the workflow above: a large pot of pasta water, or a Dutch oven of stew scraped clean. Options:

  • Cool it first, always. Wash a hot pot only if it's genuinely small. Big pots go on the counter or cart until room temperature.
  • Empty at the sink using the sprayer. Roll to the sink, tip water out with the pot's own handles rested against the sink edge — don't lift and pour. A large silicone spatula scrapes food into the trash before rinsing.
  • Soak overnight for anything stuck. Water + a squirt of soap + morning attention.
  • Line pans when you can. A sheet of parchment on a sheet pan, or a silicone baking mat, saves the cleaning step entirely.

Wiping down surfaces without laps of the kitchen

Keep cleaning supplies where you cook, not under the sink. A caddy on the rolling cart with a spray bottle, a stack of microfiber cloths, and a small dish soap. Wipe as you finish each cooking task, from farthest away to closest. The final wipe of the counter is short because you already did most of it during cooking.
